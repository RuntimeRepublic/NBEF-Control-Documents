## Why this research matters: Psalm 78

**History seen through the eyes of Scripture wins battles.**

- God commanded the fathers to make His works known to their children, "That the generation to come might know them… That they might set their hope in God, and not forget the works of God, but keep his commandments" (Psalm 78:5–7, KJB).
- The generation that forgot lost the field: "The children of Ephraim, being armed, and carrying bows, turned back in the day of battle. They kept not the covenant of God, and refused to walk in his law; And forgat his works, and his wonders that he had shewed them" (Psalm 78:9–11, KJB).

They were armed and still turned back. This research recovers the Puritan Mind so that this generation reads its history through Scripture and does not turn back.

## Finding in one line

**The KJB carried the words; the Geneva notes formed the mind.** The Puritan Mind was one tradition that moved from the Geneva Bible's notes onto the KJB's text, and was preached from New England pulpits until 1776.

## Timeline

| Years | What happened | Source |
|---|---|---|
| 1560 | First full Geneva Bible, made by English exiles in Geneva. Its notes ran to about 300,000 words, a third the length of the Bible text. | A Puritan's Mind |
| 1560–1611 | Geneva became the household Bible of English Protestants. Its notes taught lawful resistance to tyranny. Exodus 1:19 note on the Hebrew midwives: "Their disobedience herein was lawful, but their dissembling evil." | Wikipedia; Wesleyan |
| 1604 | Puritans at the Hampton Court Conference asked King James for a new translation. James objected to Geneva's notes as seditious. | Puritan Board; Wesleyan |
| 1611 | First KJB. Geneva contributed more than any other version: about 19% of the KJB text was kept unaltered (Bishops' Bible: about 4%). Early KJB editions carried no study notes. | Bible League Trust; Wikipedia |
| 1611–1644 | The two competed: the KJB was "the Bible without notes," Geneva "the Bible with notes." The last Geneva edition was printed in 1644. | Geneva Bible History; Wikipedia |
| By the 1640s | The KJB overtook Geneva in popularity in America. | Christianity.com |
| 1642–1715 | When Puritans gained power around 1642 they did not return to the Geneva text. Instead, at least eight or nine KJB editions were printed with the Geneva notes, the last in 1715. | Baptist Board (quoting a published study); Geneva Bible History; Wikipedia |
| 1646–1647 | Westminster Confession and Catechisms use KJB language. | Baptist Board (same study) |
| 1634–1776 | New England election sermons, begun by John Cotton in 1634, taught covenant, lawful authority, and the duties of rulers every year, a tradition lasting about 250 years. | Kevin Slack, *Pietas* (2026) |
| 1700–1780 | The KJV was "firmly entrenched as the monarch of American Protestant Bible versions." | Cambridge study |
| 1776 | Contemporaries named the dissenting and Puritan clergy among the chief agitators of the Revolution. | Baldwin, *The New England Clergy and the American Revolution* (via Institute for Faith & Culture) |

## What the Puritan Mind held (as preached)

- A fixed covenant between God and man, from which flowed justice, equity and natural rights (Journal of the American Revolution).
- Rulers are bound by that covenant; arbitrary rule is not lawful authority.
- Resistance to tyranny can be lawful (Geneva notes; election sermons).

## Caution for teaching

One scholar (Tom Furniss, University of Strathclyde) warns that the Geneva notes also recommend obedience and passive resistance in many places. Reading them as purely revolutionary takes selective reading. Under AGCL 000, Rule S6, this counts as a **counter-text** and must be named.
